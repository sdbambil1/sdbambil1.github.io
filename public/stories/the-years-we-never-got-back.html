The Years We Never Got Back

Silas Bambil · December 2024

On 4 December 2024, a few days before Ghana's general election, I came across a series of tweets by Weffrey Jellington (@jeffwellz) about the circumstances of young people living in different parts of the world. He reflected on employment, professional advancement, marriage, and the opportunities available to people growing up within functioning economic systems. What resonated with me was his observation that young people elsewhere could establish meaningful lives without necessarily having an uncle, aunt, or some politically influential relative to facilitate their progress. Reading those tweets immediately took me back to my first day in Prof. Shore's class.

There were approximately twenty of us from Ghana, alongside students from several other countries. As we introduced ourselves, I became increasingly aware of something I had previously understood without quite appreciating its significance. Some of our Asian classmates had already worked as portfolio managers, stockbrokers, investment bankers, and actuaries. Some had already started families. These were people of approximately our age, sitting in the same classroom, pursuing similar academic qualifications. Yet the professional experiences they brought into that classroom were substantially different from ours. Even some of our Nigerian colleagues appeared to have accumulated more substantial professional experience than many of us.

Many of the Ghanaian students had first-class degrees in Actuarial Science, a year of National Service, and relatively little professional experience compared with some of our classmates. Obtaining those degrees had required considerable effort. We had spent years studying demanding mathematical and statistical concepts, preparing for examinations, and acquiring knowledge that we expected would eventually translate into meaningful professional opportunities. Yet there we were, listening to people of approximately our age introduce themselves through experiences that many of us were still hoping to acquire. They had already worked in some of the professions we were attending graduate school hoping to enter.

It was one thing to know that employment opportunities in Ghana were limited. It was quite another to sit in a classroom and listen to introductions that made those limitations painfully apparent. Until then, it had been relatively easy to understand my professional circumstances within the context of the people around me. Many of us had experienced similar difficulties, so there was nothing particularly unusual about where we found ourselves. Suddenly, I was among contemporaries whose experiences gave me an entirely different basis for comparison. For all the academic qualifications we had acquired, I could not help feeling that we had been left behind.

Ghana has stolen so much of my youth. That was the feeling I struggled to get away from. I was not disappointed that my classmates had accomplished those things. They deserved their achievements, and I had no reason to resent them. What bothered me was wondering how differently the preceding years might have unfolded for some of us had we encountered comparable opportunities. We had not been sitting around doing nothing. We had studied, worked hard, completed our degrees, and undertaken National Service. We had done much of what we had been told would prepare us for successful professional lives. What happened after all that preparation?

There is something particularly frustrating about spending years preparing yourself for an opportunity and then spending several more years trying to obtain it. By the time you finally find somewhere to apply your knowledge, someone who completed their education around the same time may already have accumulated years of professional experience. While you are trying to enter a profession, they may be considering how to advance within it. You may eventually arrive at the same professional destination, but the intervening years could have offered you very different experiences. Those years matter, and they are not necessarily recovered simply because things eventually work out.

The differences extended beyond professional experience. Some of the people sitting in that classroom had already established families. I do not believe everyone must get married, have children, or attain particular professional milestones by a certain age. People have different ambitions, preferences, and ideas about how they want to live. However, there is a difference between deliberately postponing something because you are not interested in pursuing it yet and postponing it because your economic circumstances leave you with little choice. How many young people would make entirely different decisions about their lives if they had the financial independence to do so?

Perhaps that was why Weffrey Jellington's tweets resonated with me so much. It was the thought of being able to complete one's education, find meaningful employment, develop professionally, and make decisions about one's future without needing to establish some connection with a politically influential person. Imagine being able to concentrate on becoming exceptionally good at what you do because there is a reasonable prospect of being rewarded for it. Instead of spending your time trying to find someone who knows someone, you could spend that time acquiring experience, improving your abilities, and becoming more accomplished in your profession.

How much of a person's youth must be spent simply trying to get an opportunity to begin their adult life? It irritates me that someone could spend years acquiring demanding academic qualifications and still find themselves scrambling for crumbs from the so-called national cake. After all the examinations, the sacrifices, and the effort that went into becoming qualified, must one also become politically connected? I do not blame people who find themselves having to make such compromises. What disgusts me is the prospect of capable individuals having to organise their lives around obtaining favours from people whose attention they should never have needed in the first place.

What saddens me most is that even if our circumstances eventually improve, we cannot go back and experience those years differently. Someone who finally secures meaningful employment can begin accumulating professional experience, but they cannot necessarily recover the experience they might have acquired earlier. Financial stability may eventually afford them opportunities to travel, establish a business, support their family, or simply enjoy their independence. But there are things they might have wanted to experience at an earlier stage of their lives. I am optimistic about my future, yet that optimism does not make me any less disappointed about the opportunities I feel were previously unavailable to me.

It was against this background that I found some of the political discussions preceding Ghana's 2024 election so frustrating. References to Free SHS, Planting for Food and Jobs, and NABCO would sometimes enter conversations about the country's economic circumstances. Whatever merits people attributed to these programmes, my concerns extended beyond their existence. I was thinking about the professional opportunities young people could access, the purchasing power of their earnings, and the kind of lives they could reasonably aspire to build. People simply wanted to make something of their lives. Mentioning a government programme did not necessarily answer their concerns about what came next.

When I expressed these frustrations, some people appeared more interested in determining whether I supported the NPP or the NDC. That was what made their responses particularly irritating. I was reflecting on something I had personally experienced, something that had made me reconsider how much of my youth I had spent preparing for opportunities rather than enjoying them. Somehow, all of that could be reduced to a question about which political party I supported. Was I supposed to feel differently about what I had experienced simply because acknowledging my disappointment might be politically inconvenient? Those reactions subsequently prompted me to write Wear Your Jersey? Keep Your Jersey.

Even now, when I think about that first day in Prof. Shore's class, what stays with me is the simplicity of those introductions. Nobody needed to explain the differences between our experiences. We were all sitting in the same classroom, pursuing similar qualifications, and were roughly the same age. Yet some of my classmates were describing professional lives that many of us were still trying to begin. I left that experience wondering how differently our youth might have unfolded had more opportunities been available to us. And when I eventually expressed that disappointment, some people genuinely thought what bothered me was which political party was in power.
