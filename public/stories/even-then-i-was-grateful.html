Even Then, I Was Grateful

Silas Bambil · December 2024

I had been in America for approximately sixteen months, and graduation was approaching. Ordinarily, this should have been one of the most exciting periods of my life. I was getting closer to accomplishing something I had worked so hard for, yet I found myself worrying more about what would happen afterwards than celebrating what I had achieved. I was struggling to find employment, and much of what I had hoped would happen by then had not materialised. It was during this period that my friend RaÏsta was also feeling discouraged. In trying to encourage her, I found myself thinking about why I remained grateful to God.

One of my greatest disappointments was not securing a summer internship. I had looked forward to putting some of the things I had learnt into practice, gaining experience, and improving my chances of finding employment after graduation. When that did not happen, I began asking myself questions that I had previously given little thought to. Was I doing enough? Had I overestimated my abilities? Was I really as good as I thought I was? It was painful to find myself questioning things about myself that I had once believed with considerable confidence. I wanted an opportunity to demonstrate what I was capable of, and I was struggling to find one.

I was equally disappointed about not being able to graduate when I had originally intended. Before coming to America, I had given much thought to how the next few years of my life would unfold. I had become so attached to the dates I had assigned to my ambitions that missing them sometimes felt like losing the ambitions themselves. I found some comfort in Ecclesiastes 3:1 (NIV), which reminds me that there is a time for everything. Although I struggled to accept that my plans had changed, I wanted to believe that the things I had hoped to accomplish were not necessarily beyond my reach.

During this period, I took on jobs that I considered menial because I did not want to depend on my parents. There were occasions when I regretted some of those decisions. There was nothing shameful about the work I was doing, and I understood why I had chosen to do it. But I would be dishonest if I said that I was happy about how things had turned out. I had come to America with ambitions that meant a great deal to me. I had worked hard to prepare myself for a particular kind of career, yet much of my immediate concern was simply how to support myself.

Perhaps one of the saddest things about that period was that I sometimes avoided calling home. I loved my parents and wanted to reach a point in my life where I could take care of them rather than depend on them. Yet somehow, when things were not going well, I found myself reluctant to speak to them. My disappointment with myself was beginning to affect my relationship with the very people for whom I wanted to do well. There is something particularly sad about wanting to make life easier for the people you love and, in the process, allowing your own frustrations to distance you from them.

I had also stopped going to church. I felt disconnected, and there were days when I struggled with a sense of apathy towards things that had previously meant a great deal to me. I had not stopped believing in God. I was discouraged, and I sometimes felt tired of trying to make things work. I found comfort in Isaiah 40:31 (NIV) and its assurance that those who hope in the Lord “will renew their strength”. I loved the thought of God renewing my strength while the things that had exhausted me remained unresolved. Sometimes, what I needed most was simply the strength to continue.

It was in the middle of all this that I wanted to encourage RaÏsta. I was not speaking to her as someone whose prayers had all been answered or whose life was going particularly well. I was still looking for employment, still questioning my abilities, and still wondering what would become of some of my plans. Much of the encouragement I wanted to offer her was something I also needed to hear. Nevertheless, there was one thing I wanted her to understand about me: despite everything I was experiencing, I remained grateful to God. And I meant it.

I was not happy about failing to secure an internship or having to reconsider my graduation plans. There were decisions I regretted and circumstances I desperately wanted to change. Yet I was grateful for God's grace, His love, and the strength He had given me throughout those sixteen months. I was grateful that I still had the opportunity to pursue my education and work towards the things that mattered to me. Perhaps I had become so occupied with the things I wanted God to do for me that I had sometimes overlooked the things for which I already had reason to thank Him.

I found reassurance in the words of Jeremiah 29:11 (NIV), particularly God's promise to give His people “hope and a future”. I did not know what would happen after graduation, and that uncertainty frightened me. There were things I wanted so badly that I sometimes struggled to imagine what my life would look like without them. Yet I found comfort in believing that God had not abandoned me. He knew how much those things meant to me. I did not have to explain to Him why I was disappointed or pretend that I was less worried than I actually was.

I also held on to the reassurance of Romans 8:28 (NIV) that God works for the good of those who love Him. There were experiences during those sixteen months that I would quite happily have done without. I did not need to pretend that my disappointments were pleasant experiences to retain my confidence in His goodness. What comforted me was believing that God could work through circumstances I wished had been different. I could be honest with Him about the things that hurt, the decisions I regretted, and the opportunities I wished I had received, without feeling that my disappointment diminished my love for Him.

God has not finished with me. I believed that wholeheartedly, even when I questioned my own abilities. There were still things I wanted to accomplish, and I did not want disappointment to take away the enthusiasm with which I had once pursued them. I intended to continue searching for employment, working hard, praying, and doing what I could to improve my circumstances. I did not know how much longer I would have to wait or what opportunities would eventually become available. But somewhere beneath all that frustration was still the person who had come to America with so much excitement about his future.

When I think about what I wanted RaÏsta to understand, I realise how much of it was something I also needed to remember. I was still searching for employment. I was still disappointed about my internship and graduation plans. There were still questions about my future to which I had no answers. I would have been much happier had some of those circumstances been different. Yet I loved God, and I believed that He had been good to me throughout those sixteen months. I was not waiting for everything in my life to work out before I could thank Him. Even then, I was grateful.

Silas Bambil
