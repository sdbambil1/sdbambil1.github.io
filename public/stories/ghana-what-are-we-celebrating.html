Ghana, What Are We Celebrating?

Silas Bambil · March 2025

On 6 March 2025, as Ghana celebrated another anniversary of its independence, I found myself feeling angry, frustrated, and disappointed. I appreciate the historical significance of our independence and have enormous respect for the people whose efforts made it possible. I wish I could have spent the day simply celebrating that achievement. Instead, I found myself thinking about the circumstances of young Ghanaians and the difficulty many of them experience in building the lives they want. There was something deeply unsettling about celebrating the independence of my country while thinking about how difficult it had become for some of its young citizens to attain a little independence of their own.

What does independence mean to a young Ghanaian who has completed their education but remains financially dependent on their parents? What does it mean to someone who works throughout the month but earns barely enough to get through it? At what point does a young person begin to feel that their life belongs to them, that they can make decisions about their future without every decision becoming a question of affordability? I would never equate these difficulties with colonial rule or diminish the importance of political independence. Nevertheless, I wish more Ghanaians could experience the freedom to make meaningful choices about their own lives.

Many young Ghanaians are doing precisely what society has encouraged them to do. They study, acquire qualifications, develop their abilities, and pursue employment opportunities. Their parents make sacrifices in the hope that education will provide them with better prospects. As someone who genuinely loves learning, I find it painful to think about graduates who are eager to put their knowledge to use but struggle to find opportunities to do so. I would never suggest that education loses its value because employment is uncertain. But surely, we should be interested not only in how many people we educate but also in what those people are subsequently able to do with their education.

Even employment does not necessarily mean that someone can begin building the life they want. I would love to get married, have children, establish a comfortable home, and take care of my family. These are ordinary ambitions, yet their pursuit can involve years of financial uncertainty. Some young people are working but cannot save enough to move out of their parents' homes or confidently make plans about starting families of their own. Another month arrives, another set of expenses requires attention, and their ambitions remain somewhere in the future. What troubles me is how easily an entire period of someone's youth can become devoted to merely getting through it.

Then there are the ordinary necessities of life. Reliable electricity, decent roads, accessible healthcare, and quality education should make it easier for people to pursue their ambitions. I find it frustrating how much effort, expense, and uncertainty can accompany access to these things. Someone trying to establish a business already has enough to contend with without having to worry excessively about the reliability of electricity. Someone who becomes ill should be able to devote more of their attention to recovering than to wondering whether they can afford treatment. Life presents enough difficulties without the things that are supposed to make it easier becoming additional sources of anxiety.

Moving to America in 2023 changed how I regarded some of the circumstances I had previously accepted as ordinary. Living elsewhere made me appreciate how much the environment in which someone lives can influence what they are able to accomplish. America has certainly not spared me from professional disappointment, employment difficulties, or uncertainty. I know from personal experience that moving abroad does not automatically resolve one's problems. Nevertheless, encountering different institutions and opportunities has made me question how much more some Ghanaians might accomplish if they had access to circumstances that allowed them to make better use of their abilities.

This is partly why I have very little patience for explanations of young people's difficulties that begin and end with telling them to work harder. Anyone who knows how much I value education and personal development would understand that I am not dismissing the importance of effort. I believe in discipline, responsibility, and continually improving oneself. But hard work does not create every opportunity a person needs. I wish we spent as much time questioning whether young Ghanaians have adequate opportunities as we spend encouraging them to prepare for those opportunities. It is frustrating to watch people continually improve themselves without necessarily seeing a corresponding improvement in their circumstances.

Perhaps this explains why Japa appeals to so many young people. There is something sad about growing up in a country you love and increasingly imagining the life you want to live somewhere else. I understand that feeling because some of the opportunities I have wanted to pursue have taken me outside Ghana. Leaving does not necessarily mean that one loves Ghana any less. I would love for migration to be something young Ghanaians pursue out of curiosity, ambition, or a desire to experience the world, rather than something they feel compelled to consider because their prospects at home appear so limited.

And then I think about our parents. Many of us grew up watching them work hard and make sacrifices because they wanted us to enjoy opportunities they did not have. I genuinely look forward to being able to make life more comfortable for my family. I want my parents to enjoy some of the things they perhaps could not afford to enjoy while raising us. Yet some young people find themselves still trying to establish their own financial independence while their parents grow older. There is something painful about wanting to relieve the people you love of some of their responsibilities and discovering that you are still struggling to assume your own.

Against this background, I find much of our political drama exhausting. Another controversy, another promise, another argument about which political party deserves our affection. Meanwhile, someone simply wants a job. Someone wants to afford decent accommodation. Someone wants to take care of their ageing parents. Someone wants to start a business and earn enough to build a comfortable life. These are the things I wish occupied more of our national conversations. I would love for our enthusiasm about politics to be matched by an equally enthusiastic interest in whether the lives of ordinary Ghanaians are actually becoming easier.

I admire the resilience of the Ghanaian people. I admire those who continue working, learning, pursuing opportunities, and supporting their families despite difficult circumstances. But I sometimes wish we had fewer reasons to celebrate our resilience. I would rather see young Ghanaians devote more of their intelligence, energy, and creativity to pursuing their ambitions than to overcoming the obstacles that stand between them and those ambitions. I do not expect life to be without difficulties, nor do I expect Ghana to resolve every problem its citizens encounter. But I wish so many ordinary ambitions did not require such extraordinary perseverance to pursue.

I love Ghana. That is precisely why I find some of these things so upsetting. I want young people to be able to imagine fulfilling lives in their own country. I want them to have enough financial security to enjoy their friendships, fall in love, pursue their intellectual interests, take care of their families, and experience the simple pleasure of being young. I want them to have ambitions beyond escaping their present circumstances. Surely, there ought to be more to our youth than preparing ourselves for a life we keep postponing.

Ghana, we are tired. I wish I could have expressed something different on that Independence Day, but that was genuinely how I felt. I look forward to celebrating an independence that means more to young Ghanaians than the remembrance of an important historical achievement. I want to celebrate their growing ability to support themselves, pursue their ambitions, and build lives they genuinely enjoy in the country they call home. I want the generations born into Ghana's independence to experience more of the possibilities that independence was meant to create. That is the Ghana I would love to celebrate.

Until then, Ghana, what are we celebrating?

The original reflection that inspired this essay was written on 6 March 2025, Ghana's Independence Day.

Silas Bambil
