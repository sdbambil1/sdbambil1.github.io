The Day I Walked Again

Silas Bambil · August 2021

When I completed my undergraduate studies, I had a fairly clear idea of what I wanted the next few years of my life to look like. I would complete my national service, move to the United States, and pursue graduate studies. I had given much thought to my education and professional future, but nowhere in those plans had I accounted for the possibility of having to learn how to walk again. As it turned out, one of my greatest accomplishments the following year had nothing to do with mathematics, education, or work. I walked without crutches.

In Ghana, graduates of tertiary institutions are required to undertake a year of national service. I was initially posted to the Driver and Vehicle Licensing Authority (DVLA), but they already had more national service personnel than they could accommodate. I had to arrange a reposting and eventually secured a place at the Ghana Statistical Service. This meant moving to Aburi to stay with my uncle. Things started well. I had good bosses, some of my former classmates were also working there, and I was enjoying the experience.

On December 7, 2020, I travelled to Obuasi to vote in the general election. I needed to return to Aburi that same day so I could report to work the following morning. I remember getting to Aburi. The next thing I remember is my sister sitting by my bedside, asking me to look at my leg. I cannot account for what happened between those two memories. Somewhere within that period, I had sustained a serious injury to my left lower limb. Whatever arrangements I had made for the following morning no longer mattered.

Until then, December 7 had been an ordinary day. I was simply returning to Aburi in preparation for another working day. We make such arrangements with little thought about what might prevent us from carrying them out. In fact, we sometimes plan the next five or ten years of our lives with much the same confidence. I could not have anticipated, even that morning, what the following six months would require of me. Planning for the future remains important, but having a plan is no assurance that the future will accommodate it.

I underwent two surgeries, one of which involved external fixation. The thought of having metal pins inserted into my bones alone nearly broke me down. I eventually agreed to the procedure, although that did little to ease my fears about what would follow. Until then, I had never suffered an injury of that nature. Both surgeries were successful, but the success of an operation and the recovery of a patient are two different things. There was still the excruciating pain to endure and the uncertainty of when I would regain my mobility.

I was supposed to start walking independently earlier than I eventually did, but I struggled whenever I tried to get around without my crutches. At some point, I genuinely felt as though I had forgotten how to walk. It is strange having to learn something you cannot remember learning in the first place. For as long as I could remember, walking had required little conscious effort. I could get up and go wherever I wanted without considering what made that possible. So much of my independence depended on something I had scarcely given any thought to.

June 16, 2021, will always be a special day for me. After two successful surgeries and more than six months of recovery, I was finally able to walk without crutches. Had someone asked me at the beginning of my national service what I hoped to accomplish by June of the following year, I would probably have spoken about my education or professional development. Walking would have been such an absurd answer that neither of us would have taken it seriously. Yet, after everything I had gone through, I could hardly have asked for a better achievement.

The accident delayed my plans to move to the United States by approximately two years. Two years can seem like an enormous amount of time when you have already decided where you ought to be by the end of them. We sometimes become so attached to our preferred timelines that we lose sight of the ambitions themselves. The accident had changed when I could pursue my education, not whether my education was still worth pursuing. Abandoning that ambition simply because I could no longer realise it within my preferred timeframe would have made little sense.

We are often too quick to reach conclusions about ourselves when things are not going well. A difficult year becomes evidence of a wasted life; an unsuccessful attempt becomes evidence of an inability to succeed. Yet what happens to us over a few months or years cannot adequately tell us what is possible over an entire lifetime. Some setbacks have permanent consequences, and not everyone will recover what they have lost. Even then, a meaningful future need not resemble the one originally imagined. Falling behind our own expectations is hardly sufficient evidence that we have failed in life.

The experience also changed how I regard some of the things we worry about. We compare ourselves with friends, become anxious about what we have not accomplished, and attach enormous importance to reaching certain milestones at particular ages. Many of these deadlines are of our own making. There is no universal age by which we must complete our education, establish a career, or achieve financial success. Yet we sometimes spend years feeling inadequate because our lives have failed to conform to a schedule we drew up when we knew far less about ourselves and the world.

My fears about surgery and recovery were genuine, and I would never dismiss the anxiety of someone going through a similar ordeal. Nevertheless, some of the concerns that occupy our minds deserve far less attention than we give them. Preparing for an uncertain future is sensible, but preparation can easily become preoccupation. We can spend so much time worrying about whether tomorrow will be good enough that we scarcely notice what is good about today. Of all the difficulties I might have anticipated when I completed my undergraduate studies, the one I eventually encountered had never occurred to me.

Despite everything I went through, I believe I took more positives than negatives from the experience. I spent more time with my family, discovered things about myself that I had not previously appreciated, and was finally able to quit politics. Those months gave me an opportunity to reconsider some of the things to which I had devoted my time and energy. I would have preferred to arrive at these realisations without sustaining an injury, but I cannot deny that the experience helped me mature.

One of the things I value most about family is having people around whom I do not constantly have to prove my worth. Much of our adult lives is spent demonstrating our competence, fulfilling responsibilities, and trying to accomplish something worthwhile. During those months, however, my educational and professional ambitions had little bearing on the concern my family had for me. Nobody needed me to accomplish anything remarkable to justify caring about me. They simply wanted me to get better. It is easy to overlook the value of such relationships when we are busy trying to make something of ourselves.

If you see me walking around town today as though nothing ever happened, it is because of my family that I am able to do so. You see someone walking normally; they remember the period when I could not. Much of what appears to be an individual accomplishment is made possible by people whose contributions remain invisible to others. My recovery is no exception. I will always be grateful to my family for being there for me. I only wish we did not sometimes need unfortunate events to recognise the importance of the people we already have in our lives.

Alongside the medical care I received, I drew comfort from knowing that people were praying to the Lord on my behalf. I remain grateful to everyone who remembered me in their prayers during those difficult months. And oh, how can I forget my friends who held me down throughout that period? David, Benedict, Blaise, Adomako, and Jemima, I cannot thank you enough. You guys are the real MVPs. I would rather not relive those months, but I will always be grateful to have had you around during that period of my life.

None of this has diminished my ambition. I still believe in working hard, pursuing an education, and making the most of the opportunities available to me. I am, however, more mindful of what we sometimes sacrifice in pursuit of the things we want. We neglect our health, deprive ourselves of rest, and postpone spending time with our families until we are less busy. But when exactly will we be less busy? There may always be another qualification to obtain, another professional milestone to reach, and another reason to postpone the things we keep promising ourselves we will eventually make time for.

If you died today, your employer could have a replacement before your funeral. Your family and the people who genuinely care about you do not have that luxury. I believe in taking one's work seriously, but our responsibilities extend beyond the organisations for which we work. Resting when we need to, looking after our health, and making time for the people we love should not leave us feeling that we are falling behind. So, show up every day and do your best work, but let your health and sanity always be your first priority.

I still make plans for the future, though I am less inclined to judge my progress by the dates I have assigned to them. Some ambitions will take longer to realise, and others may eventually cease to matter. There is much to look forward to without overlooking what we already have. When I completed my undergraduate studies in 2020, I was looking forward to travelling thousands of miles to pursue my education in the United States. On June 16 of the following year, I was simply grateful to be able to walk.

Silas Bambil
