Wear Your Jersey? Keep Your Jersey.

Silas Bambil · December 2024

“Wear your jersey.” “So you’re NDC now?” “You want us to return to Mahama?” These were some of the messages I received after expressing my dissatisfaction with governance in Ghana. Apparently, determining my political allegiance was more important than addressing anything I had actually said. Those who genuinely know me already understand where I lean politically. I have never pretended to be neutral. But Massa, what exactly is so alluring about your NPP or NDC that someone must permanently attach themselves to either? Do you people understand nuance at all? Must I suspend my judgement simply because I have previously supported a political party?

The instruction to wear my jersey was particularly amusing because I am a Manchester United supporter. Goodness knows the club have given me enough reasons to reconsider that decision over the years. Yet here I am, still supporting them. I complain about their performances, question their decisions, endure the ridicule of rival supporters, and somehow convince myself that watching their next match is a sensible use of my time. Even with this rather questionable commitment to Manchester United, I have never felt obliged to defend a poor performance. Why should politicians expect more loyalty from me than my own football club?

Manchester United can ruin my weekend. A poorly performing government can ruin people's livelihoods. Decisions made by politicians can diminish purchasing power, restrict employment opportunities, undermine businesses, and frustrate years of personal effort. I can afford to be sentimental about Manchester United because my livelihood does not depend on whether they win their next match. I cannot afford to extend that same sentimentality to people entrusted with managing a country's affairs. Ghana is not a football club, and citizenship is not a supporters' association. My political support must be earned, and it must continue to be earned.

Much of my frustration stems from a conviction that extends beyond politics: I refuse to reward behaviour that I find unacceptable. Few things irritate me more than seeing people behave terribly and subsequently receive the same admiration and support they previously enjoyed. Apparently, being talented, wealthy, influential, or professionally accomplished can make certain behaviours considerably more tolerable to others. Why exactly should someone reconsider their conduct when it costs them nothing? We complain bitterly about how people behave, yet continue to give them the very things they value. Sometimes, I wonder whether we are genuinely offended by the things we claim to find unacceptable.

There are people whose work I will never patronise because of how they have conducted themselves in the past. They could become internationally recognised, produce extraordinary work, or establish themselves among the finest individuals in their respective professions. Good for them. I do not have to pretend that their work is poor simply because I dislike their behaviour. If it is excellent, it is excellent. But I am under no obligation to purchase it, promote it, recommend it, or contribute to their success. Being brilliant at what you do does not make you a brilliant human being.

Nor am I particularly impressed by the tendency to treat subsequent accomplishments as compensation for previous misconduct. An award is not an apology. Increased popularity is not evidence of remorse. I can recognise that someone has genuinely changed without feeling obliged to become their admirer, customer, or friend. Me deɛ, I have better things to do than spend my time wishing someone failure or discouraging others from supporting them. They can pursue their ambitions, enjoy their accomplishments, and receive whatever recognition comes their way. I simply want no part in it. Their success does not require my participation.

Why, then, would I make an exception for politicians? What is it about political office that should exempt someone from the standards I apply to everyone else? If anything, the responsibility entrusted to politicians gives me greater reason to scrutinise their conduct. It is exhausting to hear people complain about their economic circumstances and then become offended when someone criticises the leadership they support. Apparently, the hardship is real enough to complain about in private but becomes politically inconvenient when discussed in public. Enti, how exactly do you expect better governance when you regard criticism as an act of betrayal?

The double standards are even more irritating. Conduct that would ordinarily provoke outrage suddenly becomes defensible when the person responsible belongs to one's preferred political party. Yesterday, it was unacceptable; today, we apparently need to appreciate the complexities of governance. Tomorrow, should the opposition do precisely the same thing, it will presumably become unacceptable again. The behaviour has not changed. Only the identity of the person responsible has changed. I have very little patience for this nonsense. If you need to establish someone's political affiliation before deciding whether their conduct is acceptable, spare me the pretence that your objection was ever about the conduct itself.

Perhaps one reason I can speak so freely about politicians is that I have absolutely no ambition to build my life around pleasing one. I have never aspired to scrape for crumbs from the so-called national cake, nor do I intend to spend my life doing dirty, silly jobs for a politician who may not even know I exist. Imagine having to defend someone's foolish decisions because your prospects of obtaining a job or receiving an opportunity depend on remaining in their good graces. I would rather have less and retain my independence than earn a living at the expense of my dignity.

What disgusts me about political patronage is that people may find themselves having to make precisely that compromise. Imagine spending years acquiring an education, developing your abilities, and making sacrifices, only to discover that knowing a politically influential person might matter more than everything you have learnt. Ghana has capable, educated, and hardworking people who should not have to spend their most productive years seeking the attention of politicians. Why should someone have to demonstrate political loyalty to obtain an opportunity for which they are already qualified? After all the effort that goes into becoming competent, must one also become politically useful? What an absurd waste of human potential.

A person's salary does not become more valuable because their preferred party is in power. The cost of living does not become more manageable because they can provide an impressive explanation for why it has increased. What matters to me is whether hardworking people can improve their circumstances, whether businesses can thrive, and whether young people can build decent lives without attaching themselves to politicians. You can spend an entire afternoon explaining the complexities of governance to someone who can barely afford the things they need, but asɛm no ne sɛ, they still have bills to pay. At the end of the day, it's about the economy, stupid!

At the time of my original reflection, if you looked at the conditions in Ghana and genuinely believed that the government deserved another term, that was entirely your decision. I have never been particularly interested in telling other adults whom they should vote for. You have your own circumstances, priorities, convictions, and reasons for making your choices. In the grand scheme of things, I do not matter nearly enough to assume responsibility for your electoral decisions. So just do you, bro. But do not expect your political preferences to become obligations that I must fulfil.

What I will not do is manufacture admiration for people whose behaviour I find reprehensible. I can recognise your intelligence without admiring your character, acknowledge the excellence of your work without patronising it, and agree with your political convictions without defending your misconduct. If retaining my independence of judgement makes me a disappointing political ally, so be it. I have no desire to become the sort of person who condemns unacceptable behaviour everywhere except among the people he supports. I already have Manchester United to provide me with all the irrational loyalty I need in my life. Keep your jersey.

Silas Bambil
