And Still, I Had to Smile

Silas Bambil · June 2025

I had graduated, but I was unemployed. My academic journey had not been without its difficulties, and I was struggling with my faith. Much of what I had imagined would follow the completion of my education remained beyond my reach. Yet I continued smiling, interacting with people, and going about my life as though everything was reasonably fine. Sometimes, I genuinely enjoyed myself. At other times, smiling was simply easier than explaining how I felt. There were things about my own life that I was struggling to understand, and I did not always have the energy to explain them to somebody else.

Graduation was something I had spent years working towards. I had imagined that completing my studies would bring me closer to the independence I had always wanted. I would finally have an opportunity to put my education to use, earn a decent living, and begin making plans beyond school. I was proud of having graduated, but I found it difficult to enjoy that accomplishment without thinking about everything that was supposed to follow it. I had reached a point in my life that I had looked forward to for years, only to discover how little certainty I had about what came next.

My academic struggles during my studies had also affected me more than I would ordinarily have cared to admit. For much of my life, my intellectual abilities had been a source of confidence and personal satisfaction. I enjoyed learning and took pride in being capable of doing difficult things. Experiencing academic difficulties had made me question a part of myself in which I had always placed considerable confidence. Although I had completed my studies, some of those doubts remained. Being unable to find employment afterwards did little to reassure me, particularly when I was already questioning whether I was good enough.

What frustrated me about being unemployed was how much of my life seemed to depend on something I could not immediately change. I had academic qualifications, professional credentials, and ambitions that extended well beyond obtaining them. I wanted an opportunity to work, support myself, and begin establishing the kind of life I had imagined. Instead, I found myself wondering how much longer I would have to wait before I could do those things. I could continue learning and improving my abilities, as I had always done, but what I desperately wanted at that point was an opportunity to put those abilities to use.

I was also struggling with my faith. I love God, and my relationship with Him means an enormous amount to me. Yet there were occasions when I struggled to find the reassurance I had ordinarily derived from that relationship. I wanted to experience peace, but there were things that continued to occupy my mind. I believed in His goodness and faithfulness, even though I sometimes struggled to understand how my life was unfolding. It was particularly difficult to admit that something which had given me so much comfort over the years had itself become something with which I was struggling.

The difficulty with continually appearing fine is that it can become another responsibility in itself. Besides dealing with the things that bothered me, I was also concerned with how much of what I was experiencing became apparent to others. I did not particularly want people to know how uncertain I felt about my future or how disappointed I sometimes was with myself. That did not mean every moment of happiness was pretended. I could genuinely enjoy someone's company and still have things weighing heavily on my mind. Sometimes, however, I smiled simply because I did not know how to explain everything happening beneath that smile.

Through all of this, I felt that, as a man, I had to carry on. That was how I understood my responsibilities at the time. My disappointment would not provide me with employment, and my uncertainty would not relieve me of the decisions I needed to make. There were things I had to confront regardless of whether I felt emotionally prepared to confront them. Perhaps I placed too much pressure on myself to remain composed, but I felt that I could not afford to spend too much time attending to how I felt when there was already so much about my life that needed attention.

No one was coming to save my ass, and no one owed me shit. That was how bluntly I had come to regard my circumstances. I appreciated the people who cared about me, and I was grateful whenever someone offered assistance. But I could not spend my life expecting somebody else to assume responsibility for it. I had qualifications, but nobody was obliged to give me a job. I had ambitions, but nobody was obliged to provide the opportunities I wanted. However disappointed I was with the way things had turned out, I still had to decide what I was going to do next.

I also had to embrace my decisions and actions. There were choices I had made with considerable confidence that I subsequently questioned. Some had produced outcomes I had anticipated, while others had left me wondering whether I should have done things differently. It is considerably easier to take ownership of a decision when it produces the outcome you wanted. I found it much more difficult when I was living with consequences I had never imagined. Nevertheless, those decisions were mine. I could regret them, acknowledge where I might have been wrong, and reconsider my direction without expecting someone else to make those decisions for me.

Of course, not everything that had happened to me was necessarily the consequence of something I had done wrong. There were circumstances I could not have anticipated and things I could not have changed, however carefully I had planned my life. I understood that, but it offered only limited comfort. Regardless of how a particular difficulty had arisen, I was still the one who had to confront it. Sometimes, what I wanted most was simply to know whether the decisions I was making would lead me somewhere I wanted to be. It was lonely having to make those decisions without knowing what would become of them.

Perhaps that was why I became increasingly appreciative of my immediate family. So much of my life had become concerned with proving that I was capable of something, acquiring qualifications, and trying to convince people to give me an opportunity. With my family, I felt that I already belonged. I did not have to secure employment before belonging to them. I did not have to possess an impressive professional title or accomplish something remarkable to have a place among them. There was something deeply reassuring about knowing that, however uncertain the other aspects of my life became, I still had people whose love I believed I could depend upon.

I had spent much of my life wanting to become a man capable of taking care of his family. I wanted to provide for the people I loved rather than continue depending on them. It was painful that I was not yet in a position to do many of the things I wished to do for them. Yet the difficulties I encountered while pursuing independence made me appreciate their presence even more. I wanted to give them a comfortable life, and there was considerable comfort in believing that my inability to do so at that point did not make me any less their own.

I do not know whether I handled that period particularly well. Perhaps there were things I should have spoken about rather than concealed, and perhaps I placed more pressure on myself than I needed to. What I know is that I was struggling, even on days when I might have appeared perfectly fine. I had graduated, but I was unemployed. I was struggling with my faith, questioning myself, and trying to understand what would become of my life. There were responsibilities I still had to confront. And still, I had to smile. But somewhere in all of this was the comforting thought that, after spending so much of my life trying to become somebody, there were people to whom I was already someone.

Silas Bambil
